RGB vs CMYK: why your print looks different than your screen

Your design looked perfect on screen and came back dull or off-color in print. That's not the printer's mistake — it's RGB vs CMYK, and almost nobody teaches it because you learned design on a screen, not on a press.

RGB is light. CMYK is ink.

Your screen makes color with light — red, green and blue mixed together. That's RGB. It's additive: add all three at full and you get white. Screens, phones and cameras are all RGB, and light can make intensely bright, glowing colors.

A press doesn't shine light at you — it lays down ink on paper. Four inks: cyan, magenta, yellow, and key (black). That's CMYK. It's subtractive: each ink absorbs some light and reflects the rest back to your eye. More ink means less light bounces back, so the brightest thing CMYK can do is the paper itself.

Why the colors shift

RGB can describe far more colors than four inks can physically mix — especially bright blues, greens and neons. When an RGB file hits a CMYK press, every color ink can't reach gets mapped to the closest color it can print. That electric blue becomes a flatter blue; that neon green calms down. Nothing is broken — the press simply can't glow.

The danger: if you build in RGB and never check, the first time you see those shifted colors is on the finished print. By then it's too late — and a reprint bill.

How to fix it — before you send

Convert to CMYK in your own design app, so you control the shift instead of the press:

  • Illustrator: File → Document Color Mode → CMYK.
  • Photoshop: Image → Mode → CMYK.
  • InDesign: work in a CMYK document and export PDF/X.

Now you see the shift on your own screen and can adjust — bump the saturation, or pick a color that's actually printable — so what you approve is what comes off the press. For a deep, solid black in large areas, ask your printer for their "rich black" build rather than relying on 100% K alone.

FAQ

Will the printer just convert it for me?
Yes — but with nobody watching. The RIP picks the nearest ink colors automatically, so you don't get to see or approve the shift. Converting yourself keeps you in control.
Does RGB ever print fine?
Digital/inkjet presses are more forgiving, and neutral colors shift little. But bright, saturated colors are where RGB bites — so for anything color-critical, convert and check first.
What about Pantone / spot colors?
Pantone (spot) colors are mixed inks for exact brand colors and are separate from CMYK. If you need a precise brand color, talk to your printer about a spot color rather than a process (CMYK) build.
