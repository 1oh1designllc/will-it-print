300 DPI: why your images print blurry

Your logo looked razor sharp on screen and printed soft or blocky. The file wasn't broken — it just didn't have enough resolution for print. This is the one that catches everyone who learned design on a screen.

What DPI actually is

Every image is made of tiny dots — pixels. Resolution is how many of those dots you pack into an inch of print: DPI, dots per inch. More dots per inch means finer detail, because the dots are smaller and invisible. Fewer, bigger dots and your eye starts seeing the squares — that's what "pixelated" means. You're literally seeing the dots.

Why 72 isn't enough

Screens only show about 72–96 DPI, so an image that looks perfectly sharp on your monitor can have nowhere near enough detail for paper. A press wants about 300 DPI to look clean up close. "It looks fine on my screen" tells you almost nothing about how it'll print — screen-sharp and print-sharp are two different bars.

Resolution depends on size

Here's the part nobody explains: resolution isn't a fixed property of an image — it depends on how big you print it. The same image that's razor sharp at 2 inches can be a blurry mess at 10 inches, because you're spreading the same dots over more area.

The math: inches × 300 = pixels you need. A 4 × 6 postcard needs about 1200 × 1800 px. A 24-inch-wide poster at 300 DPI would need 7200 px — which is why large-format is allowed to print at a lower DPI (you view it from farther away).

Why you can't just enlarge it

When you scale a small image up, the software invents pixels to fill the gaps by guessing. It doesn't add real detail, so it just gets soft and mushy — there's nothing to recover that was never captured. Most low-res files come from the web, where images are small on purpose to load fast. Pull one off a website, blow it up in a flyer, and it falls apart on paper.

How to get sharp prints

  • Start from high-resolution source images — you can't add detail that isn't there.
  • Check resolution at your final print size, not just on screen (Photoshop → Image Size).
  • If an image is too small, place it smaller or find a larger version — don't force it bigger.
  • For logos and type, use vector art — it stays razor sharp at any size.

FAQ

Is DPI the same as PPI?
In everyday use, yes — DPI (dots per inch) and PPI (pixels per inch) are used interchangeably for how much detail an image has at print size. (Strictly, PPI is the image, DPI is the printer, but the practical rule is the same: aim for 300.)
Does large format really need 300 DPI?
No. Posters and banners are viewed from a distance, so 150 DPI — or even lower for huge banners — looks fine. The bigger and farther, the lower you can go.
What about vector files?
Vector art (logos, type, icons from Illustrator) has no fixed resolution — it's math, not pixels, so it stays sharp at any size. DPI only matters for raster images (photos, scans, flattened graphics).
