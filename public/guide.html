The print-ready checklist

Printers reject files for the same handful of reasons every day. Here's what "print-ready" actually means — the five things a press checks, and the exact numbers that keep your job from getting sent back. Then you can check your own file free, right in your browser.

1 The right size & trim

Every print product has a finished (trim) size — the dimensions after it's cut. A standard business card trims to 3.5 × 2 in, a letter flyer to 8.5 × 11 in. If your file is built at the wrong size, or in the wrong orientation, the art gets scaled or rotated to fit and nothing lands where you designed it.

How to get it right

  • Build your document at the exact trim size the printer lists.
  • Keep important text and logos at least 0.125 in inside the trim (the "safe margin"), so nothing critical is cut off.
  • Match orientation — a landscape file ordered as portrait will come back rotated.

2 Bleed

Presses can't cut perfectly to a hairline, so any color or image that's meant to run to the very edge must extend past the trim. That overhang is bleed. Without it, a sliver of white paper shows along the edge when the cut drifts even slightly.

Standard bleed is 0.125 in (3 mm) on every side. Large-format pieces like posters and banners usually want 0.25 in.

How to get it right

  • Set the bleed in your design app's document setup, and pull any edge artwork out to the bleed line — not just to the trim.
  • Export with crop marks (trim marks) so the printer knows where to cut.

3 Resolution

Images that look crisp on screen can print blurry. Screens show about 72–96 pixels per inch; a press needs far more detail. Resolution is measured at print size — blowing a small image up to poster size drops its effective resolution fast.

300 DPI at final size is the target for cards, flyers and brochures. 150 DPI is a safe floor for most work; large-format viewed at a distance can go lower.

How to get it right

  • Start from high-resolution source images — you can't add detail that isn't there.
  • If an image is too small, place it smaller or find a larger version. Upscaling invents pixels and still looks soft.

4 Color — CMYK, not RGB

Screens make color with light (RGB); presses make it with ink (CMYK). The bright blues, greens and neons you see on screen often can't be mixed from ink, so when a press converts RGB art it shifts those colors. Your print can come out duller or different than your monitor showed.

How to get it right

  • Build or convert your file to CMYK in your design app before sending, so you see the shift and can adjust.
  • Deep, solid black? Use a "rich black" build rather than 100% K alone for large areas (ask your printer for their recipe).

5 Embedded fonts

A PDF can name a font without actually carrying it. When it reaches a press that doesn't have that exact font, the press substitutes another one — your text reflows, spacing breaks, or a whole different typeface prints. This is one of the most common silent rejections.

How to get it right

  • Export your PDF with "embed all fonts" turned on (it's a checkbox in most export dialogs).
  • Or outline/flatten the text to curves so there's no font to miss — just note you can't edit the type afterward.

The 60-second checklist

  • ✓Size: built at the exact trim size, right orientation.
  • ✓Bleed: 0.125 in past the trim on every edge (0.25 in large-format), crop marks on.
  • ✓Resolution: 300 DPI at final size (150 DPI floor).
  • ✓Color: CMYK, not RGB.
  • ✓Fonts: embedded or outlined.
